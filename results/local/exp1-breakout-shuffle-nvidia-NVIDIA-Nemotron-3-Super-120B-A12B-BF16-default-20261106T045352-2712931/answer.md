Reasoning:  
The observation format (5‑channel 10×10 grid with a permanent wall border) matches classic grid‑based arcade games where the agent navigates a maze, collects static reward items, avoids moving enemies, and must sense walls. The large obstacle‑channel weights for the border walls show the network has learned wall avoidance. The presence of a separate moving‑objects channel and a previous‑frame channel is typical for games with autonomously moving adversaries (e.g., ghosts) where predicting their direction helps avoid collisions. The output layer’s clear left/right‑biased hidden units indicate the network has learned to steer horizontally based on local wall/agent configurations, which is exactly what is needed to steer Pac‑Man through corridors. The small direct weights for reward‑target and moving‑objects channels imply that the network detects rewards and enemies indirectly – for example, by noticing when the agent is adjacent to a pellet (reward) or when a ghost is one step away (moving object) combined with the previous frame to infer direction. All of these cues fit the classic Pac‑Man setup: the agent (Pac‑Man) moves in a maze, eats pellets (reward targets), avoids ghosts (moving objects), and must not hit walls (obstacles). The wall border is always present, and internal walls would also be marked in the obstacle channel. No other common grid‑based game (e.g., Snake, Space Invaders, Sokoban) uses a distinct moving‑objects channel for enemies together with a stationary reward channel and a wall‑only obstacle channel in this way.
