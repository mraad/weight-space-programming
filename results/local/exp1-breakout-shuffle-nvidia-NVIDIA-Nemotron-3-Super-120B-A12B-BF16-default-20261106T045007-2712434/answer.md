

Observations:  
Looking at the first‑layer weight matrix W1, the largest magnitudes appear in rows that correspond to the reward‑target channel (channel 2) and to the moving‑object/obstacle channels (channels 1 and 3) in the cells immediately surrounding the agent. For example, in the block of five rows that represent the cell just above the agent (row = agent_row‑1, column = agent_col), the channel‑2 weights show a strong positive pattern (≈ +2.0 to +4.0) while the same cell’s channel‑1 and channel‑3 weights are large and negative (≈ ‑2.0 to ‑4.0). Similar signed patterns occur for the left, right and below cells, forming a kind of “push‑pull” field: reward‑target cells excite hidden units that later drive movement toward them, whereas moving‑object and obstacle cells inhibit those same hidden units or excite units that drive movement away.  

The output‑layer weights W3 and biases b3 reveal how hidden units map to the five actions. Hidden unit 0 has large negative weights for actions 0‑3 and a small positive weight for action 4 (right), so when this unit is active it pushes the policy toward moving right and suppresses the other directions. Hidden unit 1 shows the opposite pattern: strong positive weights for actions 0‑3 and a large negative weight for action 4, thus encouraging left/up/down/no‑op and discouraging right. Other hidden units display comparable antagonistic pairs for the vertical axis (e.g., units 6 vs 21 for up/down). Together with the biases (which slightly favor no‑op and right), this creates a competitive encoder where the network can select a direction based on which sensory inputs (reward, body, walls) activate the appropriate hidden units.  

Reasoning:  
The presence of a dedicated “previous frame” channel that holds the agent’s and moving objects’ last positions gives the network a direct velocity signal, which is essential for games where the agent’s own trail is dangerous (e.g., Snake). The mixed excitatory/inhibitory patterns in channels 1 and 3 (moving objects and obstacles) indicate that the network treats the snake’s own body as both a moving thing to track and an impassable/ lethal obstacle—exactly how the body behaves in Snake. Reward‑target channel 2 shows a clear attraction signature, consistent with eating food to grow. The action encoding (no‑op, up, down, left, right) matches the discrete‑grid movement of a snake.  

Games like Pac‑Man would treat ghosts as moving dangers but not as obstacles that the agent must avoid by remembering past positions; they also usually give reward for eating pellets and occasional fruits, but the network would not need a strong inhibitory signal from the moving‑object channel in the same way it does from the body in Snake. A simple collection game with stationary rewards and static walls would not require the moving‑object channel at all. The observed structure therefore points to a game where the agent’s own trail is both moving and lethal.  

Game: Snake (the classic arcade game where the player controls a snake that continuously moves, eats food to grow, and loses upon hitting walls or its own body).  

Description: The agent is the snake’s head (channel 0). The moving‑object channel (channel 1) encodes the snake’s body segments; the reward‑target channel (channel 2) encodes food; the obstacle channel (channel 3) encodes the static walls and also the snake’s body (making collision fatal); the previous‑frame channel (channel 4) stores the last positions of head and body to give the network a sense of direction. The agent receives reward when it eats food and the episode ends when it collides with a wall or its own body.
