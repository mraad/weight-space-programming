The output‑layer biases (b₃ = [1.221, 1.008, 1.024, 0.897, 1.298]) give a slight preference for the right‑ward action (action 4) and a mild bias against left (action 3). The hidden‑to‑output matrix (W₃) contains both strong positive and negative weights (e.g., hidden unit 4 gives +3.081 to left, hidden unit 11 gives –5.336 to left and –2.100 to right, hidden unit 22 gives +1.692 to right, etc.), showing that the network can steer the hidden activity toward specific directions based on the features extracted by the first two layers.

Reasoning:  
Because the first‑layer weights are nearly identical across cells for the non‑obstacle channels, the network relies on higher‑layer combinations to extract spatial relations (e.g., whether a reward target is up, down, left or right of the agent, or whether a moving object lies in the direction of motion). The modest obstacle weights provide a constant repulsive bias from walls, encouraging the agent to stay away from the borders. The presence of a “previous frame” channel (which records where the agent or moving objects were on the last step) gives the network a short‑term memory of velocity, allowing it to infer direction and avoid reversing into its own trail. The output layer can then map hidden unit activity to concrete movement choices (up, down, left, right, no‑op) with a slight rightward bias, consistent with many trained agents that develop a preferred direction when the environment is symmetric.  
These patterns match a classic snake‑like task: the agent is a growing head, moving objects represent the snake’s body (collision ends the episode), reward targets are food items that give reward when eaten, obstacles are the surrounding walls, and the previous frame helps the snake know where its body just was so it does not run into itself. Other candidate games (e.g., Pac‑Man, pure chase, catching) either lack a growing moving‑object tail or do not use the previous frame in the same way, making them less consistent with the observed weight structure.
